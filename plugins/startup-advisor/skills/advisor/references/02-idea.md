## 1. Core idea

A strong idea is rarely a clever sentence that arrives complete. It is a starting point built from noticing a problem, understanding a group of people, and seeing a change that makes a new solution possible — and it evolves with learning. Evaluation is not a one-time verdict; it is the ongoing move from assumption to evidence. No process can guarantee success before building; the goal is a better starting point and a clear separation of what is known from what is assumed.

## 2. Where strong ideas come from

- Don't try to think up "startup ideas" directly; look for problems, ideally ones the founder has (Graham, *How to Get Startup Ideas*). Closeness to the problem improves observation and reduces guessing.
- Someone who lives the problem knows when it happens, what people do today, why current solutions annoy, and which part really hurts.
- Useful paths to ideas (Jared Friedman, YC): a problem the founder lives; deep domain expertise; a technical or behavioral change that opens an opportunity; a problem proven important in another context.
- Ideas need not be unprecedented. Competitors can prove demand. Better questions: What does this team understand differently? Which group is underserved? What change enables a better solution now?

## 3. Organic vs. manufactured ideas

- **Organic ideas** grow from real experience and problems. **Manufactured ideas** are invented because the founder wants a company and grabs anything that looks right — often starting from a template or a technology looking for a problem.
- Danger: when the solution is the only starting point, the team over-reads weak signals because it is attached to what it wants to build.
- Organic ideas carry tacit knowledge but still need validation; the founder's experience may not represent a broad market.
- Turn closeness into research: Do others have it? How often? What does it cost? What do they do today? What have they tried?

## 4. Ideas evolve

- Many companies don't keep their day-one idea (YC). Scope, customer, product, or model change through contact with users. Waiting for a complete, guaranteed idea sets a standard that contradicts startup reality.
- Not all starting points are equal: a real problem, a reachable customer, ability to build an experiment, and domain knowledge make evolution productive.
- **Deep evolution ≠ random change.** Changing direction weekly based on every opinion isn't learning. Learning needs explicit hypotheses and evidence: what we believed, what we tested, what happened, what changed in our understanding.

## 5. Problem before solution

- Common mistake: start with a solution (app, platform, AI, device, marketplace, subscription) and look for a problem to fit it. Technical capability does not prove need.
- Understand the current state first: Who suffers? What happens, when? Impact? Current alternatives? Why unsolved? Recurring or rare? Painful enough to change behavior?
- Building can itself be a learning tool — but early builds should test an important hypothesis, not stack a long chain of assumptions.
- The more attached a team is to a solution, the harder it is to see the problem needs something different. Frame early around customer and problem: Who are we serving? What are they trying to get done? What stops them?

## 6. Who the customer really is

"Customer" is often too vague. Map the roles around the decision; they may coincide or separate:

| Role | Question |
|---|---|
| Feels the problem | Who experiences the pain? |
| User | Who uses the solution? |
| Economic beneficiary | Who gains financially? |
| Approver | Who signs off? |
| Payer | Who holds the budget? |
| Blocker | Who can stop the deal (legal, security, IT, procurement)? |

A user may love the product without budget; a buyer may accept the value while users refuse to change. "A product for businesses" is not a learnable market — a five-person startup differs from a bank, hospital, or government entity. A narrow initial segment makes early learning precise; it doesn't keep the company small.

## 7. The first (beachhead) market

- MIT's *Disciplined Entrepreneurship* uses the beachhead market: focus limited resources on a segment you can understand and serve. Trying to serve everyone usually means serving no one deeply.
- A good first market has relatively similar customers in problem and context; widely different needs make it impossible to tell whether the product is improving or fragmenting.
- Focus also helps reach: knowing who to talk to tells you which places, communities, and channels to use.
- It is a strategic hypothesis, not a permanent decision; a defined starting point is what makes discovering a better segment possible.

## 8. Timing: why now?

- A real problem can still have the wrong timing; an old idea can become possible through changes in technology, cost, infrastructure, regulation, or behavior.
- Ask "why now?" (YC): new technology, cheaper compute, new APIs, shifted consumer behavior, regulatory change. Change doesn't guarantee success, but explains why a solution can work today when it couldn't before.
- Good timing also appears where new tools exist but haven't spread into a specific sector — domain knowledge determines where value is created.
- "The market is changing" is not evidence. Specify what changed and how it affects cost, quality, or willingness to adopt.

## 9. Schlep blindness

- Graham's term for an unconscious tendency not to see ideas that involve tedious, hard work — the mind avoids them before they form.
- His example: online payments, a problem many developers knew was painful; its tedium kept people away (connected to Stripe).
- Difficulty is not proof of a good idea; much hard work has no economic value. But difficulty shouldn't auto-disqualify; it can be a barrier to competitors, especially if the team is better equipped.
- Separate two questions: Is the problem important? Is the difficulty solvable?

## 10. Living at the edge of a field

- Founders close to new developments and unsolved problems see opportunities before they are obvious (Graham).
- This means direct contact with changing technology, users, or operations — not just following news.
- Working in an existing startup can build this knowledge (how products are built, customers handled, decisions made under uncertainty). The value is in the learning, not the name.
- Someone not ready to found now may prepare better by building rare expertise than by storing a list of theoretical ideas.

## 11. Competitors and alternatives

- A good idea need not have zero competitors. Successful companies in a space can prove demand; no competition can mean an undiscovered opportunity or a weak market.
- Analyze what customers use today: another product, a person doing it manually, scattered tools, a spreadsheet, or deciding not to solve it.
- Key question: why would the customer change behavior? A new product competes with the current way, including habits, learning costs, and switching risk. Technically better may not be enough.
- Successful companies in other markets or regions can show a problem supports a business, but transferring a model requires understanding local, regulatory, and behavioral differences — not copying.

## 12. Talking to users

- Talking to users is among the most important early founder work (YC). Question quality and listening determine its value.
- The goal in problem discovery is not to convince. Pitching enthusiastically then asking "would you use this?" gets politeness, not evidence.
- Ask about the past and present: How do you handle this now? When did it last happen? What did you do? What did it cost? What have you tried?
- Specific stories beat general agreement ("yes, that's a problem"). Behavior beats opinion: someone who complains but never tried to solve it gives a different signal than someone paying for an imperfect alternative.
- Listening beats defending. In Customer Discovery the goal is to learn and test hypotheses, not seek applause.

## 13. Interview anti-patterns

| Don't | Do instead |
|---|---|
| Hypothetical questions: "Would you use this?" "How much would you pay?" | Ask about the last real occurrence and what they did. |
| Present the solution at the start | Understand context first; save the solution for later. |
| Leading questions: "Isn't it annoying to do this manually?" | Open questions: "Walk me through the last time you did this." |
| Many shallow responses | Fewer deep conversations with the right segment. |
| Interview people who don't have the problem | Filter for people who live it; wrong samples create noise. |

## 14. Opinion vs. evidence

Signals are not equal. A rough spectrum, from weaker to stronger:

1. General opinion or verbal approval
2. Description of a specific past experience
3. An alternative already in use (especially a paid or costly one)
4. Commitment of time or data to a trial
5. Repeated use / voluntary return
6. Financial or contractual commitment, where appropriate

This is not a fixed law — some products need time before purchase; some markets have long sales cycles — but the closer evidence is to real behavior and real cost, the higher its value. Customer Development treats the initial vision as falsifiable hypotheses. "People want a better experience" is hard to test; a good hypothesis can be proven wrong.

## 15. Validate the problem before expanding the solution

- Validation reduces uncertainty about the most important assumptions; it doesn't prove an idea "right" once.
- First validate existence, severity, frequency, and who is affected. If the problem isn't important, improving the solution won't fix the core flaw.
- Problem validation doesn't always need a product: interviews, workflow observation, alternative analysis, simple experiments. But "research" must not become an excuse to delay facing the market.
- Each validation step should reduce a specific risk. If the biggest unknown is whether the problem exists, a beautiful interface doesn't answer it. If the problem is proven but the solution's ability to deliver is doubtful, a prototype becomes valuable.

## 16. Customer accessibility

- A real problem in a big market can still be hard for an early team to reach. Evaluate access to customers and the ability to learn from them.
- Some segments adopt fast; others need long approvals, integrations, or regulatory steps. That doesn't make them bad markets, but it changes time and resources needed.
- Direct access to real users is a big early advantage: faster learning cycles, the customer's language, first real usage.
- If the team can't find target people to talk to, the market definition may be too broad or too far from its network — a risk to understand, not an automatic disqualifier.

## 17. When a small market is a good start

- A small group that wants the product intensely can beat a broad market that doesn't care much (YC, Graham).
- A narrow first market enables a precise product, similar users, and clear understanding of why they use it.
- The problem isn't small beginnings; it's the absence of a logical growth path. Distinguish a focused first market from a final market that is too limited. Expansion is not automatic; know what can transfer to other segments.

## 18. Ambitious ideas that feel frightening

- Some ambitious ideas feel frightening because of scale or because they exceed the founder's self-image (Graham, *Frighteningly Ambitious Startup Ideas*) — a filter alongside schlep blindness.
- Ambition alone doesn't make an idea good; fear of scale doesn't make it weak. Break a big problem into a small, testable entry point.
- The difference between ambition and delusion is connection to evidence. The vision can be broad; the first step must be learnable.

## 19. AI and new technology

- New technology waves tempt teams to start from the tool. This can lead to real opportunities but risks technical demos without strong need.
- Stronger question: what is possible now that wasn't possible or economical before? Who benefits? Which problem becomes solvable differently?
- YC's AI-idea material emphasizes exploring new capabilities and noticing changing fields — but technology remains part of the hypothesis, not a replacement for understanding users.
- Technical advantage fades as tools spread. Domain knowledge, user access, data, workflow understanding, and learning speed matter alongside the model.

## 20. Eight-dimension evaluation framework

Use these as connected lenses, not an automatic score:

1. **Problem** — real, recurring, important? Impact? What do people do today?
2. **Customer** — who feels it, uses, decides, pays? Reachable?
3. **Evidence** — opinions and politeness, or behavior and commitments?
4. **Timing** — what changed: technology, cost, behavior, regulation?
5. **Team** — what knowledge, capability, or access lets this team learn and execute here?
6. **Market** — room to grow if it works? Which focused first market?
7. **Alternatives** — how is it solved today? Why would the customer switch?
8. **Testability** — is there a near-term experiment that reveals something important, or years of building before any signal?

The dimensions interact: a huge market with no real problem is weak; a strong problem may not scale; excellent technology can arrive at the wrong time.

## 21. Diagnostic questions

- Who exactly has this problem? Describe one real person.
- When did the problem last happen to them, and what did they do?
- What are they using or paying for today?
- How many people in the target segment have you spoken to? What did they *do*, not say?
- Why now — what specifically changed?
- What do you know about this space that others don't?
- What is the cheapest experiment that could prove you wrong this week?

**Good output for an interview plan:** 5–8 open questions about past behavior, no solution pitch, plus who to interview and where to find them.

## 22. Common mistakes

| Mistake | How to respond |
|---|---|
| Solution looking for a problem | Reframe around customer and job-to-be-done. |
| "No competitors, so it's a great idea" | Ask what they use today; absence can mean a weak market. |
| Treating friends' enthusiasm as validation | Place it at the bottom of the evidence ladder. |
| "Our market is everyone" | Define a beachhead segment of similar customers. |
| Pitching in interviews | Ask about the last real occurrence instead. |
| Pivoting after every opinion | Require an explicit hypothesis and evidence before changing. |
| Dismissing hard or "boring" problems | Separate "is it important?" from "is the difficulty solvable?" |
