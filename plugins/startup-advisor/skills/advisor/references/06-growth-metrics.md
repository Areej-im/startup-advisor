## 1. Core idea

Growth is not just bigger numbers. Signups can rise while old users leave at the same rate; revenue can rise while burn rises faster; transaction value can grow while the company's actual share stays small. Start not with "how do we grow faster?" but "what is actually growing, why, and does it build a stronger company?" The difficulty isn't lack of numbers but choosing the numbers that reveal the truth. The goal is a system that keeps answering: are we creating value that can be retained, repeated, and expanded?

## 2. What product-market fit really means

- PMF is often used loosely for any product with users or revenue. Some customers ≠ strong fit.
- Marc Andreessen: being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market. Michael Seibel: real fit shows when demand is clear in market behavior — customers want the product, usage or purchase moves strongly, and the company is busy keeping up with demand rather than pushing a weak product onto people.
- Not every company experiences sudden explosive demand, and operational chaos isn't a requirement. Signals vary by model: consumer — repeat return and organic growth; B2B SaaS — renewal and expansion within accounts; marketplace — repeat transactions and retention on both sides. The common thread: the market gives signals hard to explain as temporary curiosity.
- **Declaring PMF too early is dangerous** because it changes behavior: hiring, higher spend, growth teams, wider channels, fundraising on the assumption the core problem is solved. If wrong, the company accelerates resource consumption, not success.
- Treat PMF as a continuously tested hypothesis, not a phrase to celebrate.

## 3. PMF is not uniform across the market

- Fit may be strong in one segment and weak in others. Averaging segments can hide the most important signal.
- Example: mid-size companies retain and expand a B2B product; small ones leave over price; large ones stall on security and integration. The overall average looks ordinary, hiding a strong market core.
- Segment by dimensions that change the nature of value: customer size, use case, acquisition source, plan, job role, geography when relevant. Ask "which users does it retain, and why?"
- When a segment retains, pays, and expands more clearly, narrowing focus may be better than serving everyone. Expanding early to a broad audience dilutes the message and mixes conflicting needs.

## 4. From acquisition to retention

- Acquisition proves you can attract attention; retention tests whether value is enough to return or continue.
- Acquisition can be strong from a good campaign, press, network, or curiosity about new technology. Without retention, each new batch must replace those who leave, and churn grows costlier as spend rises.
- Both matter, but diagnose in order: before scaling an acquisition channel, confirm the right user reaches value worth staying for.
- Aggregate numbers hide problems: active users can stay flat because new users constantly replace leavers — looks stable, but no retained base exists.

## 5. Cohort analysis instead of averages

- A cohort is a group sharing a start point or attribute (e.g., everyone who started in January vs. February), tracked for the share still active after a period suited to the product.
- It shows whether new cohorts improve or deteriorate: higher retention after a product change suggests real improvement; lower retention after adding a channel suggests that channel brings less-suitable users.
- Build cohorts by acquisition source, customer type, use case, and plan — to find differences that drive decisions, not to produce more tables.
- **Curve shape matters as much as the number.** A retention curve starts at 100% and declines. If it keeps falling toward zero for a product meant for repeat value, that's worrying. If it drops then flattens, some users keep finding value.
- A flat curve alone doesn't prove PMF: the level may be too low, the window too short, usage seasonal, or the sample small. Interpret against product nature and compare cohorts.
- Retention isn't one number: user, customer, revenue, or transaction-volume retention. Consumer → usage retention; SaaS → customer and revenue retention; marketplace → repeat transactions and GMV retention.
- Don't pick an easy-to-measure event weakly tied to value. A login doesn't always mean benefit; define the behavior that represents continued value.

## 6. Retention vs. engagement

- Retention: did the user continue or return? Engagement: how do they use it while present?
- A payroll product used once a month can be well retained with few sessions; someone can spend hours in week one and vanish.
- Time in product ≠ value. Some products compete for attention, some for transactions, some for completing a task. In productivity tools, reducing time-to-complete can be success.
- The right metric starts from the nature of value and the natural frequency of need, not from popular metrics.

## 7. Primary KPI, North Star, and guardrails

- **Primary KPI:** the most important number for measuring company progress at a stage — revenue growth, retained active users, transaction volume, or another model-appropriate measure.
- **North Star Metric:** represents the value customers get, in a way that guides product and growth decisions — e.g., successful transactions, completed tasks, active teams that achieved the core outcome.
- They can be the same or different; don't let terminology add complexity. Know which number represents business progress, which behavior represents customer value, and how they connect.
- The primary metric needs **guardrail metrics** so it isn't achieved in harmful ways:
  - Revenue growth → watch churn, retention, margin (revenue can rise from fast new sales while old customers leave).
  - Active-user growth → watch acquisition source and activation quality (paid spend can inflate it unsustainably).
- Ask: **"What would make a rise in our primary metric bad news?"** The answer picks the guardrails.

## 8. Leading vs. lagging indicators

- **Lagging:** describe outcomes already happened — revenue, renewal.
- **Leading:** early signals that may predict outcomes — reaching a value moment, completing key setup, using a function tied to retention.
- Leading indicators allow intervention before the outcome, but need validation. If users who invite a colleague retain more, that's an important correlation — it doesn't prove forcing everyone to invite will create retention.
- **Analysis generates hypotheses; experiments test them.** Especially important when hunting a "magic moment" or "aha moment."

## 9. Activation and time to value

- Creating an account ≠ becoming a real user. Activation is reaching an experience where the user feels the core value: first analysis completed, first transaction, data source connected, first useful result, team invited when value is collective. Tie the definition to real value, not an easy formal step.
- Good acquisition + weak retention may mean the right user arrived but didn't see value fast enough.
- **Time to value:** how long until first real value. Unnecessary length raises drop-off. Options: simplify onboarding, provide a ready example, offer manual help, defer unnecessary steps. The goal isn't speed alone — the user must reach something important, not a shallow moment engineered to improve a number.
- Magic/aha moments: users who complete a certain action early may retain more. Useful signal, not proof of causation — maybe users with the greatest need are the ones who do it. Combine data, conversations, and experiments before redesigning the whole product around one behavior.

## 10. Funnel: where is the leak?

Stages fit to the product: see message → interest → visit → signup → activation → use → return → payment or renewal.

| Pattern | Likely area |
|---|---|
| Many visits, few signups | Offer or message |
| Good signups, weak activation | The experience / onboarding |
| Good activation, weak retention | Value isn't lasting or recurring enough |

The funnel doesn't explain the cause; it shows where to look. Then use observation, conversations, and experiments to find why.

## 11. Revenue quality

- Revenue is a strong signal because the customer bears a real cost, but it doesn't tell the whole story: a one-off custom deal, dependence on one customer for a large share of income, weak margins, or high cost to serve.
- Analyze quality: Recurring? Renewing? Expanding? How concentrated? Cost to serve? From which segment?
- Good revenue is evidence of a relationship you can understand and repeat, not just money in today.

## 12. GMV vs. revenue

- In marketplaces, Gross Merchandise Value (GMV) is the value of transactions passing through the platform — not the company's revenue. A platform can pass large volume and keep a small share.
- Both are useful with different meanings. Read GMV with retention, margin, and transaction quality; high volume isn't enough if each transaction consumes unsustainable support or incentives.

## 13. Revenue, burn, and runway

- **Revenue:** what comes in from activity. **Burn rate:** how fast cash is consumed. **Gross burn:** total cash expenses. **Net burn:** net decrease in cash after inflows.
- Example: spend 100k in a month, take in 30k → net burn ≈ 70k for that month.
- **Runway:** estimated months before cash runs out at current burn. Simple calc: cash ÷ monthly net burn. Reality shifts — revenue and expenses rise or fall; seasonal payments or large obligations appear. Update it and build reasonable scenarios.
- The three interact: revenue can grow while burn grows faster; burn can drop because the company stopped necessary investment; runway can look long because the plan itself can't reach the next stage.
- The question isn't only "how many months are left?" but "do we have enough time to reach the next proof point, and what must we prove before time runs out?"

## 14. Vanity metrics

- Vanity metrics aren't necessarily false; they're true numbers used as evidence for something they don't prove.
- Total signups rise even as users leave. Downloads ≠ active users. Visits ≠ value. GMV ≠ company revenue.
- Cumulative numbers are comforting because they rarely fall, but they don't show the current state.
- Test: **"If this number changed, which decision would change?"** If the team doesn't know, the number may be for show, not management.

## 15. Churn

- Churn can measure customers, users, or revenue leaving during a period.
- Customer churn and revenue churn can tell different stories: many small customers lost while revenue holds thanks to large ones, or most customers stay while a few large accounts leave with big financial impact.
- Understand who leaves, when, and why: before reaching value? at renewal? after a support issue? Is a segment more prone? Are customers from a certain channel less retained?
- Each cause leads to a different intervention. Overall churn rate isn't enough without segmentation and timing.

## 16. GRR, NRR, and logo retention

- **Gross Revenue Retention (GRR):** recurring revenue retained from a customer group after cancellations and downgrades, without letting expansion hide losses.
- **Net Revenue Retention (NRR):** adds expansions and upgrades. NRR above 100% means the existing base grew net despite cancellations and contraction, thanks to expansion by remaining customers. A strong SaaS signal.
- **Logo retention:** whether the same companies/accounts stay, regardless of revenue per account.
- One large customer's expansion can hide many small customers leaving; many accounts can stay while spending less. Comparing logo and revenue retention gives a more accurate picture. No single number explains the health of a customer base.

## 17. NPS and surveys

- NPS asks how likely a user is to recommend the product. Useful for tracking satisfaction trends or comparing similar groups if used consistently.
- Not a substitute for behavior: users may say they'll recommend and never return. Results depend on sample, culture, and timing.
- Some teams ask how disappointed users would be if the product disappeared, focusing on those who'd be very disappointed. Helpful for understanding who loves the product and why, but not a standalone PMF verdict.
- Surveys gain strength when linked to behavior: do strongly attached respondents also retain, pay, and use? Agreement between answers and behavior makes the picture stronger.

## 18. CAC, LTV, and payback

- **CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost):** cost to acquire a customer. Needs a consistent definition: ads only, or sales and marketing salaries, tools, commissions? State the method and keep it stable.
- The average can hide big differences across channels and segments — one channel excellent, another destructive. Analyze CAC by channel or segment when data allows.
- Early CAC is unstable (founders sell, data is thin). Imprecision doesn't mean ignore it; treat it cautiously and be clear about what's included.
- **LTV (Customer Lifetime Value):** estimated economic value a customer generates over the relationship — revenue, margin, retention, duration, depending on model.
- LTV can look mathematically precise while resting on weak assumptions. A company months old shouldn't treat years of projected value as fact. Better to understand the drivers: how long customers stay, margin, expansion, cost to serve.
- **LTV:CAC** shows whether expected value justifies acquisition cost, but the ratio alone isn't enough. Watch **payback period:** time to recover CAC from the margin the customer generates.
- High theoretical LTV with long payback pressures cash; the faster you scale, the more cash you must pay upfront before recovering it. Don't build big expansion on a pretty ratio from fragile assumptions — input quality and payback time matter as much as the ratio.

## 19. Unit economics

- Look at the core unit of the model: customer, order, subscription, trip, transaction. When we add a unit, what value does it generate and what cost does it carry?
- A company can grow orders while losing money on each one — growth increases losses. Or current unit economics can be weak with a clear, reasonable path to improvement through efficiency or scale. The difference matters.
- Unit economics needn't be perfect early, but need an honest understanding of drivers and a logical path to improvement.

## 20. Metrics by business model

No single dashboard fits every company.

**Consumer**
- Activation, active users, retention, frequency, referrals.
- Measurement cadence follows natural usage: don't judge a monthly product by daily return.

**B2B SaaS**
- MRR/ARR, churn, GRR, NRR, logo retention, CAC, payback.
- Contract ≠ usage. A customer may keep paying on an annual contract while usage falls, so financial churn may lag the real problem. Track in-account signals: user activity, core-function usage, team expansion, or declining engagement.
- Distinguish user from buyer: users may love the product while the budget owner doesn't see economic value.

**Marketplace**
- Track both sides separately: suppliers may retain strongly because they build income on the platform while buyers use it less often.
- GMV retention: how much of a cohort's transaction value remained or expanded over time.
- Fewer users may return while those who stay spend more; or many stay while transaction value falls. Read count and value together.

## 21. Growth from existing customers

- Not all growth comes from new acquisition. In SaaS, growth can come from account expansion: more seats, more usage, additional modules.
- Decompose revenue change into **New, Expansion, Contraction, Churn.** This shows whether the company constantly needs new customers to replace losses, or whether the base itself generates growth.

## 22. Organic vs. paid growth

- Organic: referrals, word of mouth, search, natural sharing. Paid: buying reach through paid channels.
- Organic isn't always good and paid always bad. A paid channel can be excellent if it brings suitable users with strong economics.
- Measure user quality per channel: retention, payment, CAC, and whether cost rises with scale.
- A high-volume channel isn't necessarily better if its users activate, retain, or pay less.

## 23. Benchmarks are not laws

- Some sources give reference numbers for growth, retention, NPS, or SaaS economics. Useful for comparison and asking questions, not as universal law.
- Consumer differs from enterprise B2B; daily products from seasonal; base size changes expected growth rate; differing metric definitions make comparisons misleading.
- Benchmarks are a starting point for questions, not a replacement for diagnosing the company itself.

## 24. Data quality first

- Good decisions need reliable data. If the definition of an active user changes, events duplicate, or test accounts mix with customers, metrics mislead however nice the dashboard.
- Document definitions: What is revenue? An active user? When is a customer churned? Which event represents activation? What is the retention window?
- Log any change in measurement so non-comparable numbers aren't compared.
- Definitions can change if an old one doesn't represent value — document the change and reinterpret comparisons accordingly.

## 25. Data and conversations together

- Data says what happened; conversation and observation help explain why.
- If the funnel shows users stopping at a step, watch usage or talk to them. If cohorts show a channel retains better, understand who comes from it and what they expect.
- Numbers alone lead to guessing causes; interviews alone overweight a few voices. Together they're stronger.
- Beware correlation vs. causation: users of a feature retaining more doesn't mean the feature caused retention — committed users may be the ones who use it.

## 26. Reviewing metrics as a team

- Metrics are useless if they sit in a dashboard and don't change decisions.
- Hold a regular review: primary metric → guardrails → interpretation of changes → next experiments. Ask why when numbers rise, and why when they fall.
- Don't change a metric's definition whenever the result is disappointing; stable measurement shows trends.
- A good review isn't a parade of charts. It reaches a decision: What changed? Most likely explanation? Evidence? Next experiment or action?

## 27. Metrics and investor updates

- A good update shows reality: revenue, growth, burn, runway, retention, and stage-relevant challenges.
- Don't pick metrics just because they look good to investors. The best metrics are the ones the team actually uses to run the company.
- Clarity builds more trust than impressive numbers that don't explain business health.

## 28. Growth and fundraising

- Funding doesn't fix missing PMF. It buys time, but can also let the company scale a mistake.
- Before increasing burn, know what the money should prove: accelerate a channel showing repeatability? build capacity for existing demand? remove a clear barrier to scaling? Or spend without a specific hypothesis?
- Capital is a tool. If the learning loop is weak, money can make it bigger, not better.

## 29. When growth is not the priority

Growth may not be the priority when:
- Retention is weak
- The segment is unclear
- The product is unstable
- Unit economics are destructive
- Runway is short

Here, adding users may increase support and cost without building value. Priority may be improving activation, reducing churn, focusing on one segment, or fixing the value experience. Healthy growth starts by identifying the real bottleneck, not assuming more acquisition is always the answer.

## 30. When a company is ready to scale

No perfect moment or single number grants permission, but aligned signals exist:
- A clear segment
- Good retention relative to the product's nature
- Understood value
- Recurring demand
- A channel showing repeatability
- Economics that don't look destructive
- Adequate operational and financial capacity (demand may exist but the product can't handle volume; a channel may work but runway doesn't allow uncontrolled expansion)

The decisive question: **"If we doubled what we're doing now, would we double the value or double the problem?"**

## 31. Growth diagnosis framework

Work through these with the founder:
1. Which segment shows the strongest value?
2. Does that segment retain at the product's natural usage frequency?
3. Are new cohorts improving or deteriorating?
4. Which behavior represents the user reaching value?
5. What is the primary metric for this stage?
6. Which guardrails prevent misleading growth?
7. What is the state of revenue, burn, and runway?
8. Where does growth come from, and what's the user/customer quality per channel?
9. Is churn under control? Does the base renew or expand?
10. Are CAC, payback, and unit economics on a logical path?
11. If we doubled acquisition now, would we double healthy value or double the leak?

These questions don't produce an automatic decision, but they prevent scaling on enthusiasm alone.

## 32. Common mistakes

| Mistake | How to respond |
|---|---|
| Declaring PMF from early revenue or hype | Look for aligned signals in a clear segment: retention, payment, renewal, expansion. |
| Reporting cumulative signups/downloads | Ask "which decision would this change?"; switch to cohort retention. |
| One overall average | Segment by customer type, channel, use case, plan. |
| Equating time-in-app with value | Measure the behavior that represents the job done. |
| Treating correlation as causation | Turn the correlation into an experiment. |
| Pretty LTV:CAC on months of data | Focus on drivers and payback period. |
| Scaling acquisition with leaky retention | Fix activation and retention first. |
| Raising money to "find" PMF | Define what the money must prove before increasing burn. |
| Changing metric definitions when results disappoint | Keep definitions stable; document any change. |
| Treating benchmarks as rules | Use them to ask questions, not to judge. |
