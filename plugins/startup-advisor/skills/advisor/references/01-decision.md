## 1. Core idea

Starting a startup is a decision to enter a long search under uncertainty — for an important problem, a solution people want, and a model that can repeat and scale. Enthusiasm starts the motion but does not answer the cost of commitment, the opportunity cost, or the founder's ability to learn amid ambiguity. A good decision does not remove uncertainty; it chooses the uncertainty worth entering.

## 2. What makes a startup different

- **Growth is the defining trait** (Paul Graham, *Startup = Growth*). A new business, even a tech one, is not automatically a startup. A successful local business can be excellent without being built to reach a large market fast.
- **A startup is searching** for a repeatable, scalable business model (Steve Blank). An established company mostly *executes* a known model; a startup is still *discovering* its customer, problem, solution, channel, and economics.
- Having a product does not prove a market. Early sales do not prove the model repeats.
- This is why early startups change so much: the target customer, the problem framing, features, channels, sometimes the idea itself.

**Why it matters:** someone who wants fast operational stability or a clear step-by-step path may find startup life exhausting. Someone drawn to discovery may find the ambiguity is the work itself.

## 3. Image vs. real work

- The social image — independence, leading a team, raising money — makes the decision fragile if it outweighs the actual work.
- The real work includes research, rejection, failed experiments, repeated customer conversations, operational problems, decisions with partial information, and unglamorous tasks.
- Founding is not automatically the highest-value choice for every talented person (YC). One can work on big problems inside an existing company, learn from a strong team, earn well, and build deep expertise.
- **Opportunity cost:** compare realistic paths — including odds of success and failure, learning, income, time, and obligations — not "successful startup vs. boring job."
- **Wanting to be a founder ≠ wanting to build a company.** The first is about identity; the second is about the problem and the work. When external applause disappears, only the daily work remains.

## 4. Risk without drama

- Most startups do not succeed (YC). This does not mean "don't start" or that failure is inevitable; it means hard work alone does not make success the default outcome.
- Risk is layered: the problem may not matter enough; the timing may be wrong; a small group may love it but the market is too small; demand exists but acquisition is too expensive; early interest never becomes a repeatable model.
- **The mature response is to convert unknowns into learning.** Break risk into testable assumptions: Does the problem exist? Will the customer change behavior? Can the team build it? Is there a channel? Do the economics hold?

## 5. The long commitment

- A successful startup can occupy many years of a founder's life (Graham, *Before the Startup*). Success itself extends the commitment.
- The founder's role changes repeatedly: from building product to hiring, managing, fundraising, operations, culture.
- Commitment does not mean ignoring health or relationships, or knowing on day one you'll stay a decade. It means not deciding on the illusion of a quick result.
- Problem choice matters here: it is hard to persist for years on something you don't genuinely care about or understand.

## 6. Timing

- No age or life stage is universally right. Timing depends on obligations, skills, opportunity, market, team, and tolerance for uncertainty.
- Obligations tend to accumulate and make change harder over time (Graham) — an observation about constraints, not a call to rush. An experienced professional may have knowledge, relationships, and resources a younger founder lacks.
- For students, university is a high-value space to explore: meet people across disciplines, build projects, test working with potential co-founders. Dropping out is not the point.
- Waiting until risk disappears means waiting forever. Ignoring constraints is also a mistake. Weigh the cost of starting now against the cost of delay, and what a small experiment can teach before full commitment.

## 7. Domain knowledge and curiosity

- Preparation does not always start with trying to think of a startup idea. It may start with learning important things and working on interesting problems (*Before the Startup*). Deep curiosity reveals opportunities invisible to people hunting for "startup ideas."
- Good ideas often come from problems the founder experiences or understands closely (*How to Get Startup Ideas*).
- Domain knowledge can come from personal experience, a technical project, research, volunteering, or repeated exposure — not only years of formal work.
- **Caution:** expertise becomes an obstacle when it hardens into closed certainty. It produces better hypotheses but does not exempt them from testing.

## 8. From "I want to found" to a real problem

- Costly mistake: starting from "what idea looks like a startup?" instead of "what real problem is worth solving?"
- A strong problem is important, recurring, understood, and affects an identifiable group.
- Understand current alternatives: no competitor does not mean an opportunity. The alternative may be an Excel file, a person doing it manually, or ignoring the problem.
- Moving from "I want a company" to "I understand a specific problem" is moving from identity to knowledge — the point where testable work begins.

## 9. Hypotheses, not facts

- A startup begins with a chain of hypotheses (Blank): who the customer is, that the problem is a priority, that a feature will convince, that a channel will reach them, that a price will be accepted.
- A plan is a temporary statement of beliefs, not a prediction. Make hypotheses explicit; vague statements let a team interpret any result as confirmation.
- Customer Development separates *search* from *execution*. The founder must be able to discover when they're wrong, not just be persuasive.
- This protects vision rather than killing it: vision sets direction; evidence adjusts the path.

## 10. Get out of the building

- The important facts about customers are not inside the building (Blank). Meetings, decks, and spreadsheets cannot prove what the market wants.
- Customer conversations reveal the customer's language, current behavior, costs, decision makers, and barriers to change.
- People are polite and express interest that never becomes use or payment. Behavioral evidence beats opinion: What did they actually do? What do they use now? How often does the problem occur? Did they spend time trying it? Did they return? Did they pay?

## 11. Build, launch, learn

- YC's core advice: make something people want, launch, talk to users, iterate.
- Early launch does not mean careless or unsafe. It means reaching the moment a real user can experience the core value.
- After launch, behavior is information: where users stop, what they use or ignore, whether they return or recommend.
- Founders may need to recruit and serve users manually at first (*Do Things That Don't Scale*). The mistake is building a growth machine before there is something worth growing.

## 12. Determination vs. stubbornness

- Determination is central (Graham, *What We Look for in Founders*).
- Determination is not stubbornness. A dangerous failure pattern is clinging to the *means* after evidence shows it is weak. Strong founders are firm in ambition and flexible in assumptions.
- No formula decides when to persist vs. change; clear metrics, ongoing user conversations, and measurable experiments make the decision less dependent on mood.
- Useful persistence learns. Repeating the same action without new knowledge is not persistence.

## 13. Money and investment

- Raising money is not the definition of success; not raising does not mean there is no company.
- Capital accelerates what has proven value but raises the cost of mistakes when used to scale an unproven model.
- Some companies need significant capital early (deep tech, regulation, infrastructure); others can reach strong evidence with limited resources.
- Investors do not solve an unclear market. Capital does not create real demand.
- Founding for quick wealth builds a dangerous expectation: outcomes are highly unequal and risk is high.

## 14. The team question

- A co-founder means sharing years of decisions, pressure, and uncertainty. Liking someone's skills or friendship is not enough; know how they work under pressure, handle conflict, commit, and what they expect.
- Working together on a small project reveals more than long conversations about intentions.
- A co-founder is not an absolute requirement, but going solo puts more weight on one person; a bad partnership can be worse than none.
- Ask: do we have the capabilities to build the product, learn from the market, and make hard decisions? Not every skill is needed on day one. (See `03-founding-team.md`.)

## 15. Different founder paths

| Path | Typical strength | Typical gap |
|---|---|---|
| Student | Time, flexibility, university network | Market experience |
| Employee | Deep knowledge of a real problem | Higher opportunity cost and obligations |
| Technical expert | Builds fast | Understanding users and the market |
| Business expert | Understands the customer | Technical or operational capability |

Don't copy a famous founder's biography; understand your own resources and constraints. Readiness is not complete skills — the difference is between a learnable gap and an unacknowledged one.

## 16. What readiness actually means

Readiness is not a logo, a deck, a registered company, or certainty. It is a set of assets:
- Understanding the nature of the path
- Ability to learn
- Realistic time
- Knowledge of or curiosity about a domain
- Ability to build, or to gather people who can
- Willingness to face evidence that contradicts the idea

**Order of stages:** some things precede the idea (self-knowledge, constraints, understanding what a startup is); some come with the idea (problem, user, ability to test); some come later (product, launch, measurement, growth). Don't optimize a growth strategy before users want the product; don't seek answers to everything before taking any step.

## 17. Diagnostic questions

Ask one or two of these, not all:
- Is your drive the problem itself, or the founder title?
- What is the realistic alternative you'd give up (job, study, income), and what obligations do you carry?
- Can you sustain years of uncertainty? What runway — personal savings, time — do you have?
- What real exposure do you have to the problem or domain?
- What is the smallest experiment you could run before committing fully?
- Is your idea built for growth, or is it a good business that isn't a startup? (Both are valid.)

## 18. Common mistakes

| Mistake | How to respond |
|---|---|
| Starting from "what looks like a startup?" | Redirect to a specific problem and who has it. |
| Assuming hard work makes success the default | Break risk into testable assumptions. |
| Expecting investors to fix market uncertainty | Capital accelerates proven value; it doesn't create demand. |
| Founding for quick wealth or status | Separate the identity motive from the problem motive. |
| Waiting for zero risk or perfect timing | Propose a small, time-boxed experiment. |
| Quitting everything before any evidence | Weigh obligations; test cheaply first. |
