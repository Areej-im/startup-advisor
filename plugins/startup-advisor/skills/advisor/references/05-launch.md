## 1. Core idea

Launch is not a single moment. It is the start of organized contact with reality — a series of chances to reach people and learn. Early success is not measured by noise: a small launch to ten users can be worth more than wide reach that produces no usage or learning. The first goal is a real connection between a real product and a real user, understood precisely enough to choose the next step.

## 2. What launch really means

- Launch is when the product becomes usable outside the internal build circle. It need not be public or big: private, limited, targeted, or open. What makes it a launch is that real people can experience value and take observable action.
- **Technical deployment ≠ market launch.** An app can be online and unknown; a service can be ready but never put in front of the right customer.
- A real launch has three linked elements: a usable product or experience, a specific reachable segment, and a clear message explaining why that segment should care. Missing any one makes learning harder: no users → no evidence; wrong users → noise; a vague message can stop people from trying a good product.
- Launch is not a marketing task separate from product; it's where product, customer, message, and channel meet. Learning from it may change the product, the segment, or how value is described.

## 3. Launch is not a final exam

- Fear of launching comes from believing the market will issue a final verdict on the first try. Most early products don't get broad attention anyway, which leaves room to experiment and relaunch.
- Launch to a small network, then a specialist community, then a professional segment, then a broader market — refining the message between attempts.
- "Launch again and again" (YC) turns launch from a gamble into a learning process. If an attempt is weak, analyze why: message didn't land? wrong audience? bad moment? value not shown fast enough? product doesn't solve the problem enough?
- Multiple launches don't mean random repeated launches. Each attempt must add knowledge; repeating the same message, channel, and experience without understanding results is just retrying.

## 4. Why launch before it feels perfect

- Founders are too close to their product: they know what every button should do and why complexities exist. New users have none of that context, so many comprehension and usability problems can't be found from inside.
- Early launch reveals the gap between intent and understanding: a sentence the founder finds clear means nothing to customers; a "simple" step stops most users; a secondary feature is what people value most.
- Delaying to add features can make things worse. If users don't understand or need the value, ten more features won't fix it; if the segment is wrong, improving for it raises the cost of change.
- Early launch doesn't mean ignoring safety, privacy, legal requirements, or core function quality. Medical, financial, security, and similar products may need higher bars. Avoid unnecessary perfection, not responsibility.

## 5. Silent launch

- Start with a quiet launch to a limited group, no public campaign. Goal: confirm the core experience works, users can get in and reach value, and the team can spot and fix problems fast.
- Useful when the product is very new or you want fewer variables: invite a few users who represent the target segment and follow closely.
- Don't measure by account count. Watch behavior: do they complete the core task? where do they stop? need explanation? return? ask to use it again?
- If every user needs manual help, that's not automatic failure — it's a learning source. Record the kind of help needed; repeated problems show what to fix before expanding.

## 6. Friends and family: useful within limits

- Useful for early testing of comprehension and usability; watching someone use the product can reveal friction invisible during building.
- Social closeness weakens evidence: friends may be polite or try it to support you, not from need. Don't treat their enthusiasm as market proof.
- Best value is direct observation: Did they understand it without long explanation? Where did they hesitate? What did they expect? Which words confused them?
- Then move quickly to users who represent the real problem. Stronger learning begins when someone with no obligation to support you chooses to use the product because they want the result.

## 7. Choosing first users

- First users aren't a random sample. Reach people who clearly suffer the problem, can try the solution now, and will tolerate an incomplete product because the need matters.
- They may forgive missing details but not missing value. With an important problem they accept manual steps or simple design; with a weak problem, polish won't compensate for long.
- A coherent first segment makes patterns visible; very different needs and contexts make it hard to know why the product works for one person and fails for another.
- The first market is an entry point, not a permanent boundary.

## 8. First ten customers: go find them

- YC, Stripe Atlas, and Paul Graham agree: early startups shouldn't wait for users to find them; founders usually reach first users and customers themselves.
- No strong brand, no wide referrals, no proven acquisition channel — so manual effort is normal: direct messages, calls, specialist communities, professional introductions, visiting places where users are.
- This is live market research, not a temporary trick for numbers. Every conversation reveals the customer's language, objections, priorities, and decision process — knowledge hard to transfer in a report.
- The first ten customers may change the product, message, and channel. Treat them as partners in discovering how the company reaches its market, not a mini version of future thousands.

## 9. Start with who you can reach

- It's tempting to chase the hardest customer: the biggest company, the most famous name, the deal that seems to change everything. Large customers may need long approvals, integrations, security reviews, procurement, legal terms, and time that a learning team doesn't have.
- Starting with the most reachable customers isn't choosing a weak market; it's choosing faster learning cycles. A customer who decides fast and tries the product in a real context can teach more than an enterprise needing months before any use.
- Large companies may become right later, once you know what you sell, to whom, the value you prove, and what the sales process looks like.
- A big deal isn't better evidence if it consumes the company or forces customization that doesn't serve the rest of the market. Does it move you toward a repeatable model, or turn you into a custom project for one client?

## 10. Founder-led sales

- Early selling can't be fully understood from outside. When the founder sells, they hear objections directly, see where conversations stall, and learn which description creates interest or hesitation.
- YC's first-customers and sales materials, and Steve Blank, stress that founders or early leadership must stay very close to validation and sales. Delegating sales before understanding it can mean hiring someone to sell something the team hasn't yet proven how to sell.
- Founders can change the product. If customers say a barrier blocks purchase, the founder links that directly to a product decision — a crucial fast loop between market and build.
- The goal isn't for the founder to own every deal forever, but to first learn a transferable path: who is the customer, how do we reach them, what's the message, objections, steps, timeline, and why do some close and others don't?
- Founder-led sales is learning, not just persuasion. It tests hypotheses: Is the problem a priority? Is the value understood? Who decides? Budget? What blocks adoption? What outcome makes the product important?
- Customers often don't buy the technical feature; they buy less time, less risk, more revenue, or a simpler process.
- The same objection from suitable customers is information. If every deal needs a completely different story, the segment may be too broad or the value unclear.
- Good early selling doesn't hide product weakness; it tests whether there's enough value to move the customer to real action.

## 11. Describe the product in one sentence

- A key clarity test: can the team describe the product in a simple sentence a target-segment person understands? It needn't explain all the technology or future vision — just what this is, for whom, and the core value.
- Vague descriptions waste attention. "A revolutionary AI-powered platform redefining the future" sounds strong but tells the user nothing about what they'll get.
- A clear description names the segment or context, the problem or job, and the main result. It can change with learning — the message is a hypothesis about how the market understands the product, not a sacred slogan.
- If it takes minutes before someone knows what the product does, simplify. If they understand but don't care, the issue may be value importance or segment fit, not wording.

## 12. Message, value, and channel

When a launch underperforms, don't assume it's always the product:
- **Message:** the right people saw it but didn't understand the value.
- **Channel:** the message is good but reached the wrong audience.
- **Product:** the right users understood, tried it, and didn't find enough value.

Separating these matters. Rebuilding the product after launching in the wrong channel may be hasty; endlessly changing ads for a product that doesn't retain users postpones facing the value problem. Track the path: who saw the message, showed interest, started, reached value, returned, paid — and where the biggest drop happens.

## 13. Launching inside communities

- Each suitable community can be an independent launch opportunity: professional groups, specialist forums, user groups, university networks, industry gatherings, tech communities. The point isn't posting a link everywhere but reaching circles where people have the problem.
- Communities react badly to shallow promotion. Understand their rules, language, and needs; present the product in a useful, clear context.
- A small community can produce higher learning than a wide ad — members share context, making feedback more comparable.
- Success in one community doesn't prove the channel scales. The founder may be known there, or the community may be unusual. Distinguish a channel that delivered first users from one that can repeat economically.

## 14. Do things that don't scale

- One of the most common unscalable things early on is recruiting users manually (Graham). Founders go to users.
- Examples: setting up accounts manually, helping each user personally, doing part of the service behind the scenes, continuous one-to-one contact. Inefficient compared with a big company, but sensible during search.
- The goal is learning and an excellent experience for first users. Serving a few closely shows details dashboards don't: why a user stopped, what they tried, what they expected.
- Unscalable work isn't a permanent goal. Watch for repeated patterns that can become product or process. The value is using manual work to discover what deserves automation.

## 15. Exceptional customer service

- Early companies can give attention big companies can't: the founder replies personally, follows up, watches usage, fixes blockers fast.
- This attention is a way to understand customers, not only please them. Every support issue may reveal a product, message, or expectation flaw. If the same question recurs, the product may not explain itself.
- A small group that loves and relies on the product can be a stronger base than many indifferent users. Love shows in behavior: return, use, referral, tolerating temporary gaps, real annoyance if the product disappeared.
- Focusing on early users' satisfaction doesn't mean implementing every request. Understand why it's asked and whether it reflects a shared problem or a special case.

## 16. Ask for payment early

- Payment is one of the strongest forms of commitment when the model requires it. Praise is easy; allocating budget reveals a different level of value.
- Asking early surfaces questions free usage hides: who owns the budget, how approval works, what price seems reasonable, what objections arise, what result the customer needs to see.
- Not every product must charge from minute one — some rely on networks, free usage, or long sales cycles. But if your hypothesis is that customers will pay, move toward testing it rather than settling for admiration.
- In enterprise sales, seek a real commitment that fits the stage: a paid pilot, a contract, a letter of intent with clear terms, internal resource allocation, or an actual purchase step. **Signal strength rises with the cost of backing out.**

## 17. Interest is not commitment

- Comfortable post-launch signals — likes, signups, positive replies, meetings — have some value but don't equal use, return, or payment.
- Build an evidence ladder: seeing a post < visiting the site < starting a trial < reaching value < one-time use < returning when return is expected; and "we'll buy" < a real purchase step.
- The right commitment depends on the product: a once-a-year app isn't measured by weekly return; an enterprise product may take months to close. Interpret behavior in context.
- Don't equate curiosity with value.

## 18. B2B vs. B2C launches

- **B2C:** can reach many people fast; signals include activation, return, and sharing. Easy signup doesn't mean easy habit-building or retention. Often relies more on communities, content, invitations, or suitable distribution channels.
- **B2B:** fewer customers, deeper relationships, multiple roles (user, manager, buyer, security, IT, procurement). Understanding the decision process is part of the launch. Often starts with direct messages, calls, demos, and limited pilots.
- No single channel is right for every company. The mistake is copying a tactic from a different model without understanding why it worked. Launching a consumer app differs from selling software to a bank.

## 19. Big deals early on

- A large customer can bring revenue and credibility but also risk: special features, long integrations, consuming terms. Dependence on one customer can turn the product into a custom service.
- Not a reason to refuse large companies — sometimes they're the right market from the start. Know the deal's cost and what you'll learn.
- Useful question: if this deal succeeds, did we learn a method repeatable with similar customers, or build something only this customer wants?
- Deals often slip or fail. Don't build the whole plan on an unclosed promise; the pipeline needs more than one opportunity, especially with long cycles.

## 20. Channels: where to launch

- A channel is how the product reaches the customer: direct outreach, community, content, partnership, app store, outbound sales, referrals, and more.
- Early on you don't need ten channels; test a few you can understand. A good channel brings suitable people who convert to use or purchase, not just visits.
- Evaluate: Are customers there? Can you reach them repeatedly? Time and money cost? Does the message work? Do customers from this channel actually use the product?
- A manual channel can be excellent early even if it won't scale; its first job may be getting users and learning. Later, seek more repeatable channels.

## 21. Measuring after launch

- Without measurement, launch becomes impressions. Decide beforehand what you want to know: message comprehension? starting usage? reaching a value moment? return? payment?
- Metrics follow the hypothesis. Visits aren't enough if the question is usage; signups aren't enough if most people never reach the core function.
- Map a simple path: message exposure → interest → start → activation → use → return → commitment or payment. Not every product has the same stages, but seeing the drop-off locates the problem.
- Quantitative data needs qualitative notes, especially with small numbers. "Half the users stopped" doesn't explain why; watching usage and talking to users can.

## 22. The first week

- The first week after launch is not for waiting. Monitor usage, talk to users, fix critical blockers.
- Ask: who actually used it, reached value, stopped? What repeated? Did people need explanation? Did they return unprompted? Share it with others?
- Don't change course after every single opinion; look for patterns. One note matters if it reveals a serious bug, but direction shouldn't swing with every comment.
- Combine speed and discipline: fix what blocks the test, record hypotheses, watch behavior, then decide what changes.

## 23. When to relaunch

- Relaunch when there's a real new reason: an important product improvement, a new segment, clearer message or positioning, a new channel, or learning that strengthens the offer.
- No need to wait for a huge change; each suitable community can be a new launch opportunity. But a relaunch must carry something to learn, not just reposting.
- Keep a launch log: audience, message, channel, result, learning. Over time, launch becomes an understandable process rather than a mysterious event.
- Repeated launches still need focus. Testing ten segments, ten messages, and ten channels at once makes it impossible to know what worked. Change a few variables each time.

## 24. When a launch fails: diagnosis

Weak results usually come from one of four places, plus timing:

| Where | Symptom | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | Reached people who don't have the problem | A better message won't save it |
| Message | Right audience, didn't understand the offer | Rework the description |
| Product | Understood, tried, found no value | Closer to the product or solution |
| Channel | Value exists but reach is rare or costly | Channel doesn't fit |
| Timing | Seasonal need or event-driven; interested but can't buy now | Re-time, keep the relationship |

Diagnosis needs data and conversations, not a single assumption. The question isn't "did the launch fail?" but "at which stage did people stop, and what's the evidence for why?"

## 25. Press is not a substitute for customers

- Media coverage can help some companies but isn't always the best early use of time; it may bring brief visits that don't convert, or a broad unsuitable audience.
- Conferences, awards, and partnerships can help, but evaluate them against the company's goal. If they consume time needed for customers and product, they become distraction.
- The strongest early signal is suitable people using the product and finding value. Fame doesn't replace that.
- PR is a possible channel, not proof of a strong company.

## 26. Launch and retention

- A new user isn't enough if the product assumes repeat use and nobody returns.
- A campaign can bring many people, but if they vanish after first use, more acquisition spend amplifies the problem.
- Retention isn't the right metric for every product in the same way; understand the natural frequency of need. When return is expected, voluntary return is one of the strongest value signals.
- Launch isn't the end of the funnel; the real question is what happens after arrival. (See `06-growth-metrics.md`.)

## 27. From first users to a repeatable channel

- Early customers may come from a personal network or manual effort. That's a start, not proof of a repeatable growth engine.
- Ask: Can we find similar customers the same way? Does the message work consistently? Is the sales cycle understood? Is reach cost reasonable?
- Repeatability arrives when the process depends less on luck and unique personal relationships.
- Building a growth machine before understanding value is premature. There's no point scaling a channel that brings users who don't stay or pay.

## 28. The founding team's role

- Launch tests the team as much as the product: fast decisions on scope, message, customers, support, and measurement.
- Agree on the launch goal: testing usability? finding first paying customers? testing a channel? Different goals inside the team produce different interpretations of results.
- Founders must stay close to users; knowledge shouldn't disappear into reports or intermediaries.
- Launch belongs to marketing, sales, and product together; it reveals the relationship among them.

## 29. Seven-question launch framework

1. **Who are we launching for?** A segment specific enough to reach and understand.
2. **What value do we promise?** A clear, understandable message.
3. **What can be used now?** A real experience that allows behavior, not just a description.
4. **How will we reach them?** A suitable channel or community, not waiting to be discovered.
5. **What behavior do we want to see?** Signup, use, return, payment, or another fitting commitment.
6. **How will we learn?** Data, observation, conversations, direct follow-up.
7. **What will we do after the result?** Improve, relaunch, change message, adjust segment, or revisit the product.

This framework doesn't make launch an equation; it keeps launch from being an event without a clear question.

## 30. Diagnostic questions

- Who exactly did you launch to, and how did you reach them?
- Describe the product in one sentence — would a target user understand it?
- Of the people who saw it, how many started, reached value, returned, paid?
- Where is the biggest drop-off, and what's your evidence for why?
- Who are your next 10 reachable customers, and how will you contact each one personally?
- Have you asked anyone to pay? What happened?

## 31. Common mistakes

| Mistake | How to respond |
|---|---|
| Treating launch as one big reveal | Plan a series of small launches, each with a learning question. |
| Waiting for perfection | Launch the core value safely; perfection isn't the bar. |
| Counting friends' praise as validation | Move to users with no obligation to support you. |
| Chasing the biggest logo first | Start with reachable customers who decide fast. |
| Hiring sales before the founder has sold | Founder sells first to learn a transferable path. |
| Vague, buzzword-heavy description | One sentence: what, for whom, core value. |
| Blaming the product for a channel problem (or vice versa) | Diagnose audience → message → product → channel. |
| Spamming communities | Learn the community; contribute in context. |
| Celebrating signups/likes | Climb the evidence ladder: use, return, payment. |
| Changing direction after each comment | Look for patterns; fix only critical blockers immediately. |
